A practical SEL guide for educators

Helping children grow into confident, connected people.

Social and emotional development begins before birth and continues through every stage of childhood. Educators can create predictable, caring environments that help students recognize emotions, build relationships, solve problems, and regulate their behavior.

Why early experiences matter

Prenatal development and early childhood experiences do not determine a child’s future, but they help shape the conditions in which later social and emotional skills develop.

Prenatal foundation

Prenatal health, nutrition, stress, substance exposure, medical care, and environmental conditions can influence fetal development. Supportive prenatal care and healthy relationships are protective foundations. Educators can respond by connecting families with appropriate health, nutrition, and community resources rather than treating development as fixed.

Early childhood experiences

Responsive relationships, consistent routines, opportunities to play, language-rich interactions, and safe environments help children practice attachment, communication, emotional expression, and self-regulation. Significant adversity and toxic stress can disrupt learning, behavior, and health, which makes early support especially important. Shonkoff et al. (2012)

Development is connected. Skills in one area can influence later development in another. Masten and Cicchetti describe these spreading effects as developmental cascades. A child who receives support for communication, relationships, or emotional regulation may gain skills that also help with learning and peer relationships over time.

Three stages at a glance

Use these milestones as general developmental guides, not checklists or diagnostic tools. Children develop at different rates and cultures shape how social and emotional skills are expressed.

👶

Infancy · 0–1

Social/emotional milestones

  • Forms attachments with familiar caregivers.
  • Responds to voices, faces, and social interaction.
  • Uses crying, gestures, facial expressions, and sounds to communicate needs.
  • Begins to show different emotions and responds to caregiver soothing.

Two classroom strategies

💬

Serve-and-return

Respond consistently to coos, gestures, eye contact, and sounds. Narrate what the infant is doing.


🧸

Predictable routines

Use consistent greetings, transitions, songs, and caregiving routines to build security and trust.

🧒

Early Childhood · 3–8

Social/emotional milestones

  • Names and communicates basic emotions.
  • Begins to take turns, share, cooperate, and follow group expectations.
  • Develops friendships and awareness of others’ feelings.
  • Improves ability to wait, manage frustration, and recover with adult support.

Two classroom strategies

🎭

Feelings through play

Use puppets, stories, role-play, and emotion cards to practice identifying feelings and choosing responses.


🤝

Cooperative routines

Use partner jobs, turn-taking games, and small-group problem solving to practice empathy and communication.

🧑‍🎓

Adolescence · 12–18

Social/emotional milestones

  • Develops a stronger sense of identity and personal values.
  • Places increasing importance on peers, belonging, and relationships.
  • Builds more complex perspective-taking and empathy.
  • Works toward greater independence in decision-making and emotion regulation.

Two classroom strategies

🗣️

Structured discussion

Use low-risk check-ins, discussion protocols, and scenarios that let students practice listening and perspective-taking.


🧭

Goal-setting

Have students set one academic and one personal goal, identify obstacles, and plan coping strategies.

Self-regulation: from co-regulation to independence

Self-regulation is the ability to manage attention, emotions, thoughts, and behavior in ways that support a goal or situation. Examples include calming down after frustration, waiting for a turn, shifting attention, asking for a break, and using a strategy instead of reacting impulsively.

1

Model it

Think aloud: “I am getting frustrated, so I am going to pause, take two breaths, and try one step at a time.” Show students that adults use regulation strategies too.

2

Make it visible

Post visual schedules, emotion scales, breathing cards, calm-down menus, and step-by-step routines. Practice them when students are calm, not only during a crisis.

3

Practice and reinforce

Notice the process: “You recognized you were overwhelmed and asked for a break.” Gradually reduce prompts as students become more independent.

When a student is dysregulated
  • Stay calm and reduce unnecessary language.
  • Prioritize safety and connection before academic demands.
  • Offer a quiet space, movement, breathing, water, or another known coping tool.
  • After the student is calm, briefly reflect on what happened and practice a plan for next time.
Remember

Regulation is a skill that develops through repeated practice and supportive relationships. Avoid treating a dysregulated student as intentionally “bad.” Look for the need behind the behavior while maintaining clear, respectful boundaries.

Partnering with families

Families know their children best. Offer strategies that fit everyday routines and invite families to share what works at home.

Infancy

  • Respond consistently to sounds, gestures, and facial expressions.
  • Use songs, talking, reading, and face-to-face play.
  • Keep sleep, feeding, and transitions as predictable as possible.

Early Childhood

  • Name emotions during everyday moments.
  • Use a calm-down routine such as stop, breathe, name the feeling, and choose.
  • Read stories together and ask, “How do you think the character feels?”

Adolescence

  • Have regular low-pressure check-ins instead of only talking when there is a problem.
  • Help teens set goals, plan for stress, and identify trusted adults.
  • Respect growing independence while maintaining clear safety expectations.

Adolescent safety matters

Educators and families should take statements or signs involving suicide or self-harm seriously. Follow school safety procedures, involve the appropriate school mental-health professionals, and do not promise secrecy. In the United States, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is available by call or text at 988. For immediate danger, call 911 or seek emergency care.
